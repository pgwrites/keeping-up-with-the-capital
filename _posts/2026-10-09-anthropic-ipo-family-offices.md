## The Big Sip

# Anthropic will put a public price on frontier AI before OpenAI does
{: .take}

[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/)'s IPO prospectus, seen by Reuters, shows what a frontier lab costs to run. Revenue grew 12-fold in 2025 to [nearly $4.6 billion](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/anthropics-ipo-prospectus-shows-sweeping-ai-vision-surging-costs-2026-09-28/). The operating loss was $8.06 billion. So for every $1 of revenue, Anthropic spent about $2.75. It has also committed $518 billion to cloud and computing over the coming years, more than 100 times last year's revenue. Bloomberg reports the roadshow could start [as soon as the week of 9 November](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/anthropic-said-to-target-mega-ipo-before-thanksgiving-holiday).

[OpenAI](https://openai.com/) chose the other route. It delayed its IPO (Sam Altman says [not in 2026](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/openai-seeks-30b-at-1-4t-valuation-as-ipo-waits)) and is reportedly raising [at least $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/openai-targets-30-billion-in-new-funding-at-1-4-trillion-value), 64% above its March price. Dealroom puts a $30 billion round in the top 1% of more than 1,250 late-stage US enterprise software rounds.

You might expect investors to prefer the lab that stays private and keeps raising at higher prices. In [Newcomer's September survey](https://reports.newcomer.co/) of 25 VCs, they didn't: 76% said they would buy Anthropic shares, against 40% for OpenAI. The same survey found 17% think an AI bubble probably bursts soon.

Until now, a small group of private investors has set the price of frontier AI. In November, the public market gets a vote. [Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/p/anthropics-ipo-cant-wait-forever) called the coming weeks <mark>"very instructive in assessing the health of the AI business."</mark> Whatever public investors pay for Anthropic becomes the number OpenAI's $1.4 trillion gets measured against.

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### This week's biggest AI valuation jumps are offers, not closed rounds

- [Etched](https://www.etched.com/) designs AI chips. It was valued at $10.3 billion in July and $21 billion in September. TechCrunch reports it's now fielding [offers at $40–50 billion](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/05/etched-fields-funding-offers-at-40b-valuation-sources-say/), nearly five times its July price. It has $1 billion in customer orders.
- [Kalshi](https://kalshi.com/) runs a regulated prediction market. It was valued at $22 billion in May, and Reuters says it's now in [talks at about $40 billion](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/tiger-global-dragoneer-talks-invest-in-kalshis-new-1-billion-fundraise-sources-2026-09-29/). It handled [$60.7 billion in trades in September](https://defirate.com/prediction-markets/volume/kalshi/), 78% of the market, though CNBC [found clusters of near-identical trades](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/30/kalshi-polymarket-trading-volume-scrutiny.html) in some of its products.
- [FieldAI](https://www.fieldai.com/) builds the software that runs robots. It was valued at $2 billion last year and has now signed a term sheet [at $10 billion](https://www.businessinsider.com/robotics-startup-fieldai-raises-at-10b-valuation-in-new-round-2026-10), with more than $135 million in revenue and signed contracts.

The money is also concentrating. Crunchbase counted [a record 27 rounds of $1 billion or more in Q3](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/q3-2026-global-startup-funding-ai-billion-dollar-rounds-exits-data/), which together took about a third of the $159 billion raised worldwide. Indian capital is in the same trade: Premji Invest co-led a [$145 million Series C](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-cyber-real-estate-instinct/) in CScale, a US company that makes optical links for AI servers.

A private price is only proven when someone can sell at it. PitchBook counted [just 18 US IPOs in Q3](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q3-2026-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor). So most of these prices haven't been tested by anyone outside the room. Anthropic's listing will be the first real test.

</div>

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### A $30 million-revenue AI startup sold for more than Airtable

- [Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/) [agreed to buy](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/salesforce-signs-definitive-agreement-to-acquire-listen-labs/) [Listen Labs](https://listenlabs.com/), which uses AI agents to run customer research, for a reported $2 billion. That's about 67 times its roughly $30 million in annual revenue. Listen Labs [turned down a signed term sheet](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/09/ai-research-startup-listen-labs-scrubbed-a-1-5b-funding-round-for-salesforce-talks/) from Menlo Ventures at $1.5 billion to sell.
- Bending Spoons, which buys established apps such as Evernote and Vimeo, bought [Airtable](https://www.airtable.com/) for [$1.28 billion in cash](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/), about [2.7 times revenue](https://dealroom.co/resources/airtable-bending-spoons/). Airtable earns more than 15 times what Listen Labs does, and was valued above $11 billion in 2021.
- Five weeks later, Bending Spoons agreed to buy [Miro](https://miro.com/) for $1.355 billion, [90% below its $17.5 billion peak](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/09/10/bending-spoons-buys-miro-at-90-discount-as-saas-unicorns-sell-for-scraps/).
- [EliseAI](https://www.eliseai.com/), which automates the admin work of rental housing, raised at [$4 billion](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/29/3370681/0/en/eliseai-raises-350-million-at-4-billion-valuation-to-bring-ai-deeper-into-housing-and-healthcare-operations.html), about 20 times its $200 million-plus in annual recurring revenue. Value Add VC's Trace Cohen says it earned that [<mark>"on usage, not narrative"</mark>](https://valueaddvc.com/pulse/eliseai-350m-round-4b-valuation-2026).

That's a 25-fold gap in revenue multiples between Listen Labs and Airtable. Size doesn't explain it, since Airtable is far bigger. What buyers are pricing is the expected growth rate. A young AI product is priced on where it could be. A 2021-era SaaS company is priced like a mature business that throws off cash, which is exactly the kind Bending Spoons buys.

For software founders, that changes what a company is worth. Revenue sets the floor. The growth story sets the price.

</div>

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### As AI models get cheaper, the fight moves to who owns the customer

Pranav Pai, founding partner at Bengaluru's 3one4 Capital (about $570 million in committed capital), thinks the AI labs are moving into their own customers' businesses: [<mark>"The most profitable move in artificial intelligence may no longer be selling intelligence."</mark>](https://inc42.com/resources/selling-intelligence-was-the-warm-up-for-ai-owning-vertical-markets-may-be-the-play/) His examples: Anthropic launched Claude Design after partnering with Figma, and Claude for Legal after featuring Harvey as a customer. OpenAI now sells Presence, its own support agent, after Intercom built its agents on OpenAI's models.

State Street Global Advisors argues the opposite. In a [September note](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/intermediary/insights/beyond-deepseek-chinas-2026-model-wave-and-the-repricing-of-the-ai-stack), it says the lead among model makers changes hands so often that their moats are getting thinner, and that <mark>"enterprises and software deployers, by contrast, should keep a rising share of the surplus."</mark> Theory Ventures' Tomasz Tunguz points to the pressure behind both views: model prices have [fallen 41% while usage rose 50%](https://tomtunguz.com/what-if-the-models-are-commoditized/).

Both sides agree on one thing: the model alone is becoming a weak moat. They disagree on who benefits. If Pai is right, the safe ground is deep industry knowledge the labs can't see from outside. If State Street is right, it's owning the customer relationship. Either way, a thin product on top of someone else's model is the worst place to be.

India has more at stake than most. Pai notes that Indian software firms and the country's 1,900-plus global capability centres are among the heaviest users of these models, which, in his view, shows the labs exactly how their clients work.

</div>

## India, Inc.

### India's largest startup round of Q3 had no VC in it
{: .take}

Electric-scooter maker [Simple Energy](https://www.simpleenergy.in/) raised [about $180 million in a Series C](https://inc42.com/buzz/from-simple-energy-to-arivihan-indian-startups-raised-over-233-mn-this-week/), the largest Indian startup round of the quarter. It was led by the family office of A. Velumani, founder of the diagnostics chain Thyrocare, with another family office and a few angels. That one round is about 6% of everything Indian startups raised in Q3, which came to [just under $3 billion](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-28-oct-3-12618694), down from $3.5 billion in Q2 and $3.87 billion in Q1.

Why would family money lead where VCs didn't? A VC fund has to return its investors' money within a fixed fund life. A family office doesn't. Sharrp Ventures' Rishabh Mariwala put it this way: <mark>"With a family office, it is a marriage without a divorce date."</mark> For a capital-heavy business like EV manufacturing, that patience is worth a lot.

This isn't a one-off. Indian family offices have taken part in more than 1,300 startup deals since 2014, and their number grew from 45 in 2018 to about 300 in 2024, [according to Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/magazine/india-built-a-start-up-ecosystem-on-foreign-capital-family-offices-may-now-have-to-keep-it-rolling). As VC money shrinks, founders of capital-heavy Indian companies now have a second source of growth capital, and it's domestic.

### Indian chip startups are being funded by VCs, corporates and the government together
{: .take}

[Agrani Labs](https://www.agranilabs.com/) builds AI computing hardware and software for data centres. Four former Intel and AMD engineers founded it in Bengaluru in 2024. It came out of stealth in February with an [$8 million seed round](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/indian-chip-startup-agrani-labs-emerges-from-stealth-with-8m-funding-round/) from Peak XV. Eight months later, it's reportedly raising [₹800–850 crore from Samsung, 360 One and Peak XV](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/technology/agrani-in-talks-to-raise-rs-800-850-crore-from-samsung-360-one-peak-xv/articleshow/134622670.cms), more than ten times its seed round, and applying for another ₹120–130 crore from the government's research and innovation (RDI) scheme.

Chip companies need years of funding before they ship. Agrani's round splits that burden three ways: a VC, a strategic corporate and the state. VCs including Accel, Lightspeed and Bessemer have also joined [DeepVerse](https://indianstartupnews.com/news/accel-meta-intel-and-ather-join-30-member-deepverse-platform-to-boost-indias-deeptech-ecosystem-12629528), a 30-member group set up to shape rules for slow-to-build sectors and feed into the same ₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme. If Agrani's round closes with the government money attached, other Indian deeptech founders will have a template to copy.

### Indian IPO investors are paying for scale, not brand: Moneyview soared, Snapdeal's parent sank
{: .take}

Digital lender [Moneyview](https://moneyview.in/)'s ₹1,092 crore IPO was subscribed about 100 times, and the stock [opened more than 60% above](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-28-oct-3-12618694) its ₹34 issue price.

Four days later, Snapdeal's parent AceVector drew 4.93 times demand for its ₹420 crore IPO. It [listed 11.6% below](https://inc42.com/buzz/snapdeal-parent-acevector-lists-at-11-6-discount-on-bse/) its ₹32 issue price and closed its first day 18.5% down. The company was once valued at $6.5 billion, and its FY26 loss narrowed 64% to ₹45.5 crore. Investors still passed, and Inc42's [analysis](https://inc42.com/features/snapdeal-makes-it-to-d-street-but-wheres-the-moat/) shows why: Snapdeal had 12.16 million annual transacting users in FY26. Meesho had 264 million.

A narrowing loss and a famous name weren't enough. Three more companies are heading to market:

- StockGro, an investing-education app, has filed confidentially for an IPO of [up to ₹2,500 crore](https://inc42.com/buzz/from-simple-energy-to-arivihan-indian-startups-raised-over-233-mn-this-week/), ₹800 crore of it in new shares.
- Tonbo Imaging, a defence-tech company, has SEBI's approval for a listing in which existing shareholders [sell up to 1.81 crore shares](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:613c6053f815e:0-defence-electronics-maker-tonbo-imaging-receives-sebi-approval-for-ipo/). The company itself raises nothing.
- Kuku, an audio-series app, [turned profitable in FY26](https://inc42.com/buzz/ipo-bound-kuku-technologies-turns-profitable-in-fy26-revenue-surges-6x-to-%e2%82%b91484-cr/) on ₹1,484 crore of revenue and is preparing to list.
