## The Big Sip

# Anthropic will put a public price on frontier AI before OpenAI does
{: .take}

Picture two climbers on the same mountain. One stops, sets up base camp and lets everyone see exactly how much oxygen it has burned. The other keeps climbing, funded by a small group of backers who agree on a price among themselves.

That's Anthropic and OpenAI right now. Both are frontier AI labs, the companies building the most advanced AI models. Anthropic makes Claude. OpenAI makes ChatGPT.

[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) is the one at base camp. It's preparing to go public, and its IPO filing, [seen by Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/anthropics-ipo-prospectus-shows-sweeping-ai-vision-surging-costs-2026-09-28/), gives us the first real look at a frontier lab's finances. Revenue grew 12x in 2025, to nearly $4.6 billion. But spending grew faster. Anthropic lost $8.06 billion running the business. For every $1 that came in, about $2.75 went out.

It has also promised to spend $518 billion on cloud and computing power over the next ten years. Bloomberg says it could start pitching its IPO to investors [as soon as the week of 9 November](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/anthropic-said-to-target-mega-ipo-before-thanksgiving-holiday).

A lot of people will see those numbers and flinch. I read them like the numbers of any company building infrastructure before demand fully arrives. The spending always comes first. The real question is whether revenue catches up.

[OpenAI](https://openai.com/) took the other path. CEO Sam Altman says there will be [no IPO in 2026](https://app.dealroom.co/news/note/openai-seeks-30b-at-1-4t-valuation-as-ipo-waits). Instead, OpenAI is reportedly raising [at least $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-09-29/openai-targets-30-billion-in-new-funding-at-1-4-trillion-value) from private investors.

You'd expect investors to prefer the lab that stays private and keeps marking itself up. But they don't. In September, tech newsletter Newcomer [surveyed 25 VCs](https://reports.newcomer.co/). Three in four said they'd buy Anthropic shares. Only two in five said the same of OpenAI.

So far, a small group of private investors has set the price of frontier AI. In November, the stock market gets a say. [Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/p/anthropics-ipo-cant-wait-forever) called the coming weeks <mark>"very instructive in assessing the health of the AI business."</mark> Whatever public investors pay for Anthropic becomes the yardstick for OpenAI's $1.4 trillion, whether OpenAI likes it or not.

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### This week's biggest AI valuation jumps are offers, not closed rounds

A price and a bid are different things. A bid is someone saying "I'd pay this." A price is someone actually paying it. This week brought a lot of very loud bids:

- [Etched](https://www.etched.com/) designs chips built specifically for AI. In July, it was valued at $10.3 billion. Now TechCrunch reports it's getting [offers at $40–50 billion](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/05/etched-fields-funding-offers-at-40b-valuation-sources-say/), about four to five times as much. It does have something to point to: $1 billion in customer orders.
- [Kalshi](https://kalshi.com/) runs a regulated market where people trade on the outcomes of real-world events. In May, it was valued at $22 billion. Now it's [in talks at around $40 billion](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/tiger-global-dragoneer-talks-invest-in-kalshis-new-1-billion-fundraise-sources-2026-09-29/). It handles [most of the trading](https://defirate.com/prediction-markets/volume/kalshi/) in its market. But CNBC [found clusters of near-identical trades](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/30/kalshi-polymarket-trading-volume-scrutiny.html) in some of its products, which raises questions about that volume.
- [FieldAI](https://www.fieldai.com/) builds the software brains that run robots. Last year, it was valued at $2 billion. Now it has a signed deal [at $10 billion](https://www.businessinsider.com/robotics-startup-fieldai-raises-at-10b-valuation-in-new-round-2026-10), backed by more than $135 million in revenue and signed contracts.

Zoom out, and the money is piling into a few giant deals. Crunchbase counted [a record 27 rounds of $1 billion or more](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/q3-2026-global-startup-funding-ai-billion-dollar-rounds-exits-data/) from July to September. Together, they took about a third of all the money startups raised worldwide. Indian money is part of it, too. Indian investor Premji Invest co-led a [$145 million round](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-cyber-real-estate-instinct/) in CScale, a US company that makes optical links for AI servers.

But a price only counts once someone can sell at it. PitchBook counted [just 18 US IPOs](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q3-2026-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor) in those same three months. Most of these valuations have only been agreed by the people already in the room. Anthropic is the first to walk out the door and find out what everyone else thinks.

</div>

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### A $30 million-revenue AI startup sold for more than Airtable

Two software deals show how differently buyers now price companies.

- [Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/) [agreed to buy](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/salesforce-signs-definitive-agreement-to-acquire-listen-labs/) [Listen Labs](https://listenlabs.com/), a young startup that uses AI agents to run customer research. The price was reportedly about $2 billion. Listen Labs makes about $30 million a year, so that's roughly 67 times its revenue. Listen Labs even [walked away from a $1.5 billion funding offer](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/09/ai-research-startup-listen-labs-scrubbed-a-1-5b-funding-round-for-salesforce-talks/) to take the deal.
- Bending Spoons, a company that buys established apps like Evernote and Vimeo, bought software company [Airtable](https://www.airtable.com/) for [$1.28 billion in cash](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/). Airtable makes more than 15 times what Listen Labs does. Yet it sold for [less than three times its revenue](https://dealroom.co/resources/airtable-bending-spoons/). In 2021, it was valued above $11 billion.
- Five weeks later, Bending Spoons agreed to buy software company [Miro](https://miro.com/) for $1.355 billion. That's [90% below its peak value](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/09/10/bending-spoons-buys-miro-at-90-discount-as-saas-unicorns-sell-for-scraps/).
- Meanwhile, [EliseAI](https://www.eliseai.com/), which automates the admin work of rental housing, raised money at a [$4 billion valuation](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/29/3370681/0/en/eliseai-raises-350-million-at-4-billion-valuation-to-bring-ai-deeper-into-housing-and-healthcare-operations.html), on more than $200 million in yearly revenue. Investor Trace Cohen of Value Add VC says it earned that [<mark>"on usage, not narrative."</mark>](https://valueaddvc.com/pulse/eliseai-350m-round-4b-valuation-2026)

Size can't explain the gap, because Airtable is the far bigger company. **Salesforce bought a slope. Bending Spoons bought a cash flow.** A young AI product is priced on how steeply it could grow. An older software company is priced like a mature business that throws off cash, which is exactly what Bending Spoons likes to buy.

For software founders, that's the game now. Revenue sets the floor. The growth story sets the price.

</div>

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### As AI models get cheaper, the fight moves to who owns the customer

Think of AI as a three-layer cake. The AI models are at the bottom, the apps built on them are in the middle, and the customer sits on top. The big question for the next five years is which layer gets to eat.

Pranav Pai, founding partner at Bengaluru VC firm 3one4 Capital, thinks the AI labs are coming for the top layer: [<mark>"The most profitable move in artificial intelligence may no longer be selling intelligence."</mark>](https://inc42.com/resources/selling-intelligence-was-the-warm-up-for-ai-owning-vertical-markets-may-be-the-play/) His evidence: Anthropic launched its own design tool, Claude Design, after partnering with design software company Figma. It launched Claude for Legal after featuring legal AI startup Harvey as a customer. And OpenAI now sells its own customer-support agent, after support software company Intercom built its agents on OpenAI's models.

Asset manager State Street Global Advisors sees it the other way. In a [September note](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/intermediary/insights/beyond-deepseek-chinas-2026-model-wave-and-the-repricing-of-the-ai-stack), it argues that the lead among AI labs changes hands so often that none of them can hold on to it. That leaves more of the profit for the companies using AI. Investor Tomasz Tunguz of Theory Ventures shows the pressure on the labs: the price of using AI models has [fallen 41%, while usage rose 50%](https://tomtunguz.com/what-if-the-models-are-commoditized/).

Both sides agree on one thing: owning a model alone is becoming a weak defence. If Pai is right, the safe ground is deep industry knowledge the labs can't see from the outside. If State Street is right, it's owning the customer relationship. Either way, the worst position is a thin product built on top of someone else's model.

India has more riding on this than most. Pai points out that Indian software firms are among the heaviest users of these models. So are the more than 1,900 global capability centres in India, the in-house tech offices that big foreign companies run here. In his view, that shows the labs exactly how their clients work.

</div>

## India, Inc.

### India's largest startup round of Q3 had no VC in it
{: .take}

India's biggest startup round from July to September didn't include a single venture fund.

Electric-scooter maker [Simple Energy](https://www.simpleenergy.in/) raised [about $180 million](https://inc42.com/buzz/from-simple-energy-to-arivihan-indian-startups-raised-over-233-mn-this-week/). The round was led by the family office of A. Velumani, who founded the diagnostics chain Thyrocare. Another family office and a few angel investors joined. A family office is a private firm that invests one wealthy family's money.

Indian startups raised [just under $3 billion](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-28-oct-3-12618694) in the quarter, less than in each of the two quarters before. This one round was about 6% of that.

Why would family money step in where VCs stepped back? Time. A VC fund has a deadline: it must return its investors' money within a fixed number of years. A family office has no such deadline. Rishabh Mariwala of Sharrp Ventures put it beautifully: <mark>"With a family office, it is a marriage without a divorce date."</mark> Building EVs takes a lot of money for a long time, so that patience is worth a lot.

And this isn't a one-off. The number of Indian family offices investing in startups grew from 45 in 2018 to about 300 in 2024, [per Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/magazine/india-built-a-start-up-ecosystem-on-foreign-capital-family-offices-may-now-have-to-keep-it-rolling). As VC money shrinks, Indian founders building capital-heavy companies now have a second, homegrown source of money. That's a big deal.

### Indian chip startups are being funded by VCs, corporates and the government together
{: .take}

[Agrani Labs](https://www.agranilabs.com/) builds computer chips and software to run AI in data centres. Four former Intel and AMD engineers founded it in Bengaluru in 2024. In February, it came out of stealth with an [$8 million seed round](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/indian-chip-startup-agrani-labs-emerges-from-stealth-with-8m-funding-round/) from Peak XV.

Eight months later, it's reportedly raising [₹800–850 crore from Samsung, 360 One and Peak XV](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/technology/agrani-in-talks-to-raise-rs-800-850-crore-from-samsung-360-one-peak-xv/articleshow/134622670.cms). That's more than ten times its seed round. It's also applying for ₹120–130 crore from the government's Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) scheme.

Chips are hard to fund because companies need years of money before they ship anything. Agrani's answer is to share that load three ways: a VC, a large corporate and the government. It isn't alone. VC firms including Accel, Lightspeed and Bessemer have joined [DeepVerse](https://indianstartupnews.com/news/accel-meta-intel-and-ather-join-30-member-deepverse-platform-to-boost-indias-deeptech-ecosystem-12629528), a 30-member group set up to shape the rules for slow-to-build industries. It also feeds into the same ₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme. If Agrani closes its round with the government money attached, other Indian deeptech founders get a template to copy.

### Indian IPO investors are paying for scale, not brand: Moneyview soared, Snapdeal's parent sank
{: .take}

Two IPOs, days apart, ended very differently.

Online lender [Moneyview](https://moneyview.in/) raised ₹1,092 crore in its IPO. Investors applied for about 100 times the shares on offer. On its first day of trading, the stock [opened more than 60% above](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-sep-28-oct-3-12618694) its IPO price.

AceVector, the parent of online marketplace Snapdeal, raised ₹420 crore. Demand was weaker: investors applied for about five times the shares on offer. Then the stock [listed 11.6% below](https://inc42.com/buzz/snapdeal-parent-acevector-lists-at-11-6-discount-on-bse/) its IPO price and ended its first day 18.4% down. Snapdeal was once valued at $6.5 billion, and it cut its losses sharply last year. But investors still passed.

Inc42's [analysis](https://inc42.com/features/snapdeal-makes-it-to-d-street-but-wheres-the-moat/) explains why in one comparison. In FY26, about 12 million people bought something on Snapdeal. On its rival Meesho, 264 million did.

The lesson for India's IPO pipeline: a famous name and a smaller loss won't carry you. Scale will. Three more companies are lining up to test that:

- **StockGro**, an app that teaches people to invest, has filed confidentially for an IPO of [up to ₹2,500 crore](https://inc42.com/buzz/from-simple-energy-to-arivihan-indian-startups-raised-over-233-mn-this-week/). That means its draft papers aren't public yet.
- **Tonbo Imaging**, a defence electronics maker, has approval from market regulator SEBI to list. In its IPO, only existing shareholders [will sell shares](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:613c6053f815e:0-defence-electronics-maker-tonbo-imaging-receives-sebi-approval-for-ipo/). The company itself raises nothing.
- **Kuku**, an audio-series app, [turned profitable in FY26](https://inc42.com/buzz/ipo-bound-kuku-technologies-turns-profitable-in-fy26-revenue-surges-6x-to-%e2%82%b91484-cr/) on ₹1,484 crore of revenue and is getting ready to list.
