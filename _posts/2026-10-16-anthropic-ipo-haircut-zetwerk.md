Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about the investor day that took half a trillion dollars off the table.

Last week I said the public market was about to get a vote on what frontier AI is worth. I didn't expect the vote to start before the polls opened. This week, the same money that has been marking AI up in private started asking harder questions in public: about growth, about debt, and about who actually gets paid when the models run. *So is AI getting cheaper, or just getting priced properly?*

## The Big Sip

# Anthropic grew 45% in one quarter, and investors still knocked $500 billion off its IPO price
{: .take}

Being fast and speeding up sound like the same thing. They aren't, and this week the difference cost [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) about $500 billion on paper.

On Wednesday, Anthropic hosted its biggest backers for an investor day with CEO Dario Amodei and finance chief Krishna Rao. Per [Reuters](https://wtvbam.com/2026/10/15/exclusive-investors-temper-anthropic-ipo-valuation-expectations-over-revenue-growth-slowdown-sources-say/), it told them third-quarter revenue was $16.8 billion, up 45% from the second quarter, and that it was profitable on an adjusted basis. By any normal yardstick that's remarkable. Growth of 45% a quarter compounds to more than 4x a year, and one quarter now brings in more than three and a half times the $4.6 billion Anthropic made in all of 2025.

Investors heard something else. Revenue had more than doubled from the first quarter to the second. Now it grew 45%. People familiar with the matter told Reuters that some current and prospective investors are discussing a valuation of about $1.5 trillion, down from the roughly $2 trillion they had expected for the November share sale. That's a quarter off before a single share has traded.

**Investors weren't pricing speed. They were pricing acceleration.**

Do the arithmetic and the cut looks less dramatic. Annualise the third quarter and Anthropic is running at about $67 billion. Two trillion dollars was about 30x that; $1.5 trillion is about 22x. Even the lower number is still about 55% above the [$965 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/two-exit-doors-anthropic-2-051103937.html) Anthropic was valued at in May.

The mood around it didn't help. US chip stocks lost [$1.64 trillion over six sessions](https://www.aol.com/articles/us-chip-stocks-set-wipe-135038000.html) before Thursday, which Reuters put down to bets on an interest-rate hike and worries about the cost of the AI build-out. A week earlier, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI's annualised revenue was [about $50 billion, not the $70 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/article/anthropic-q3-revenue-climbs-to-168-billion-report-221619202.html) investors had been working with.

Not everyone flinched. An early Anthropic backer told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/15/anthropic-investor-ipo-financials/) he expects the IPO to price at around $1.8 trillion and trade up to $2.3–2.4 trillion: <mark>"It will continue to be one of these names where people say, 'I feel like I have to own it.'"</mark> At the other extreme, independent research firm New Constructs [puts Anthropic's value at $150 billion](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/10/62239452/anthropic-ipo-could-be-2026s-most-ridiculous-offering-as-analyst-values-ai-firm-at-just-150-billion-unprecedented-test-of-investor-gullibility).

Here's why this matters beyond one company. Every private AI price, including OpenAI's $1.4 trillion, is about to be measured against a public one. This week, that measuring stick got shorter.

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Public investors dumped AI's landlords while private investors bid up its tenants

Remember the three-layer cake from last edition: models, apps, customers? This week the market sliced AI a different way, by who owns the hardware and who owes the debt.

- **The landlord.** CoreWeave owns GPUs and rents them out. It carries about [$50 billion of debt](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/investing/2026/10/15/coreweave-shares-bonds-slide-further-as-ai-debt-fears-mount/) against the roughly $13 billion of revenue analysts expect this year. Its shares fell more than 15% on Wednesday, its 2031 bonds now yield above 16%, a level that usually signals distress, and its credit default swaps imply a nearly 63% chance of default within five years, up from about 30% a year ago. The same day, Gartner said about 80% of neoclouds could shut down by 2030.
- **A tenant.** [Modal](https://modal.com/) rents computing power from more than 20 cloud providers and sells it to developers as ready-to-use AI infrastructure. It raised a [$750 million Series D led by Accel at $15.75 billion](https://modal.com/blog/1-billion-annualized-run-rate-series-d), with Kleiner Perkins joining, after its annualised run rate passed $1 billion. That's [more than 3x its May price](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/14/ai-cloud-startup-modal-raises-750m-at-15-75b-valuation/).
- **A toll booth.** Ramp, which runs corporate cards and spending software, raised [$1.85 billion at $60 billion](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/ramp-reaches-60b-valuation-as-it-pushes-deeper-into-ai/), co-led by Dragoneer, Thrive Capital and Founders Fund, among others. That's up 36% from $44 billion in June, and 30x its more than $2 billion in annualised revenue. It now sells a card built for AI agents, and early investor Keith Rabois told TechCrunch that Ramp will be the main way companies manage what they spend on AI tokens.

Here's the part I find fascinating. All three bet on the same AI demand. If that demand cools, the landlord still owes its lenders. Modal's capacity sits on other companies' balance sheets, and Ramp sits on top of company spending, whoever the supplier is. **This week, the market paid for the meter, not the machine.**

The bull case for the landlord is real. Evan Schlossman of Neostellar Capital, a listed VC fund that owns CoreWeave shares, told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/16/coreweave-investor-explains-ai-neocloud-debt/) its contracts are <mark>"take-or-pay"</mark> deals with <mark>"extremely high-credit-worthy customers"</mark>, and CoreWeave says its backlog more than covers its debt. But PitchBook's John Atkins summed up the week to [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/15/ai-coreweave-bonds-stocks): <mark>"people are taking a dim view of speculative borrowing that will need to be refinanced at punishingly high rates."</mark> For now, the money is moving up the stack, away from whoever carries the hardware debt.

</div>

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Siemens, AMD, Intel and Hitachi are writing the cheques for AI's physical bottlenecks

On the same Wednesday, three startups fixing three different physical limits on AI raised money, and in each case an industrial giant was in the round.

- **Power.** GPU clusters can swing their power draw in milliseconds. Estonia's Skeleton Technologies builds supercapacitor-based systems that smooth those swings. Siemens led a nine-figure round that values it [above $1 billion](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/skeleton-technologies-surpasses-1b-valuation-with-siemens-backing-for-us-expansion/), and the partnership covers power delivery from the grid to the server rack. CEO Taavi Madiberk's line: <mark>"In AI infrastructure, supercapacitors are becoming the new semiconductors."</mark>
- **Networking.** UK-based Salience Labs makes photonic chips that use light to switch data between GPUs across racks. Its [$125 million Series B](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/salience-labs-secures-125m-to-scale-photonic-chips-for-ai-data-centres/) was led by Bullhound Capital, with AMD Ventures and Intel Capital joining as new investors.
- **Materials.** Bengaluru's Scimplify turns lab processes into high-purity specialty chemicals and critical materials, made through more than 500 partner factories. Hitachi Ventures led its [$25 million round](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/asia/hitachi-ventures-leads-25m-strategic-round-in-chemicals-platform-scimplify/), with Henkel and Teknor Apex joining and Accel and 3one4 Capital returning. Teknor Apex will also be a US manufacturing partner. Scimplify runs at $200 million in annualised revenue, and when a Japanese chip-materials company wanted a source outside China, its sample passed testing in two months against an expected year.

Last edition, it was Samsung lining up behind Indian chip startup Agrani Labs. The pattern is that each backer is buying into a bottleneck in its own business. Siemens sells electrical systems for data centres, and Markus Grabmeier, its president of electrical products, said <mark>"Skeleton's portfolio perfectly complements Siemens' comprehensive data centre offering."</mark> A VC buys a return. A corporate also buys a place in the queue.

**VCs are betting on AI demand. Corporates are buying seats at its bottlenecks.** With chip stocks falling, the corporate cheque is starting to look like the steadier one for hardware founders. India has a real shot here: Hitachi Ventures' Radhika Ananth says much of the country's manufacturing capacity is underused and needs the science and quality systems global buyers require, which is exactly the gap Scimplify is filling.

</div>

## India, Inc.

### Zetwerk is listing below its last private price, and its VCs are selling more shares, not fewer
{: .take}

Here's the Indian mirror image of the Anthropic story. Anthropic's backers are arguing about how much to knock off a price. Zetwerk's backers have already accepted a lower one, because there's a real buyer at the door.

Manufacturing company Zetwerk opened its ₹5,012 crore IPO on 15 October. At the top of its price band, it's worth ₹25,583 crore, about 5% below the ₹27,000 crore post-money valuation of its March round, [per Mint](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:5d5270b1f7158:0-zetwerk-targets-rs-25-583-crore-valuation-in-rs-5-012-crore-ipo/). Mint notes it's flat once you strip out a ₹1,500 crore employee stock pool. Either way, no premium.

And the sellers want more of it. The offer for sale grew from about 9.7 crore shares in the draft to [14.45 crore](https://inc42.com/buzz/zetwerk-raises-%E2%82%B92251-cr-from-anchor-investors-ahead-of-ipo/), about 49% more. Lightspeed and Peak XV are each selling more than 2 crore shares, alongside Accel India and Kae Capital. Buyers are lined up: the anchor book raised ₹2,251 crore from Capital Group, BlackRock, Norway's sovereign fund, ADIA and 22 Indian mutual fund houses. The business is improving, too. June-quarter revenue rose 55% to ₹4,118 crore, and the net loss narrowed to ₹733 crore.

Defence-tech maker Tonbo Imaging shows the same plumbing. Its ₹852 crore IPO is entirely existing shareholders selling, and it was [subscribed 4x on day one](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/tonbo-imaging-ipo-subscribed-4-times-on-day-1-led-by-strong-nii-demand-126101501609_1.html), even though FY26 revenue fell 22.7%. Before the IPO, existing investors sold ₹413 crore of shares at ₹700 each, the top of the band, to buyers including SBI funds, HDFC Life and 360 ONE's defence fund.

Now compare the private side. Indian startups raised just [$113.35 million](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-oct-5-oct-10-12662210) in the week to 10 October, down 72% from the week before. Next week, Jio Platforms opens a [₹30,213 crore IPO](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/ipo-corner/story/jio-platforms-ipo-price-band-announced-at-rs-1065-1119-set-to-become-indias-biggest-public-issue-561665-2026-10-14), set to be India's largest ever. In India right now, the deep pool of growth money isn't in VC funds. It's on Dalal Street, and much of it is domestic.

**A slightly lower price you can sell at beats a higher one you can't.**

### India's ₹1 lakh crore deeptech fund has picked its first 19 VCs, and it wants private money alongside every rupee
{: .take}

Last edition, Agrani Labs was applying for money from the government's research and innovation (RDI) scheme. This week, the Economic Times reported who will hand most of that money out.

The RDI Fund named its first cohort: [19 fund managers sharing ₹7,131 crore](https://www.inkl.com/news/blume-speciale-kalaari-chiratae-others-part-of-first-cohort-to-receive-rs-7-131-crore-rdi-fund), picked from more than 160 applicants, per the Economic Times. Fourteen are VC funds, including Chiratae, Blume, Kalaari and Speciale Invest. Five are research organisations: IISc's ARTPARK, C-CAMP, IIT Madras Research Park, BITS Pilani's PIEDS and IIT Bombay's SINE. They can back only research-heavy companies at technology readiness level 4 or above, and they are expected to raise private money alongside the government's.

Here's how I think about it. **The government isn't picking startups. It's picking the people who pick startups.** That's the right instinct for a state that doesn't want to run a VC desk. The matching rule matters even more: it makes private LPs say yes before public money moves, and the government's commitment won't all be released up front.

The first cohort is about 7% of the ₹1 lakh crore corpus, so this is a start, not a flood, and a second cohort is promised soon. The counter-voice is worth hearing: the same ET report notes an Indian Express investigation raised conflict-of-interest questions about project selection at the Technology Development Board, another arm of the scheme. If the RDI money moves on time and the fund managers find matching capital, Indian deeptech gets patient money with a VC's discipline attached.
