Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about the investor day that took half a trillion dollars off the table.

Last week, I said the stock market was about to get a say in what frontier AI is worth. I didn't expect it to start before the IPO. This week, investors who have happily paid up for AI in private began asking tougher questions. Is growth slowing? Who's carrying the debt? And who actually gets paid when AI runs? *So is AI getting cheaper, or just getting priced properly?*

## The Big Sip

# Anthropic grew 45% in one quarter, and investors still knocked $500 billion off its IPO price
{: .take}

Moving fast and speeding up sound like the same thing. But they aren't, and this week, the difference cost [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) about $500 billion on paper.

Anthropic is the AI lab behind the Claude chatbot, and it's preparing to go public. On Wednesday, it gave its biggest investors an update, [Reuters reported](https://wtvbam.com/2026/10/15/exclusive-investors-temper-anthropic-ipo-valuation-expectations-over-revenue-growth-slowdown-sources-say/). From July to September, it brought in $16.8 billion. That's 45% more than in the three months before. It also made a profit, by its own adjusted measure.

To put that in context, Anthropic made $4.6 billion in all of 2025. It now makes more than three times that in a single quarter.

So why the long faces? The trend. In the previous quarter, sales had more than doubled. This quarter, they grew 45%. That's still fast, but it's slower. According to Reuters' sources, some investors now want to value Anthropic at about $1.5 trillion in its IPO. Before this, they expected about $2 trillion. That's $500 billion less, before a single share has traded.

**Investors weren't pricing speed. They were pricing acceleration.**

Even $1.5 trillion is a steep price. It's about 22 times what Anthropic now earns in a year. And it's still well above the [$965 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/two-exit-doors-anthropic-2-051103937.html) investors valued it at in May.

The wider mood didn't help. US chip stocks lost [$1.64 trillion in value](https://www.aol.com/articles/us-chip-stocks-set-wipe-135038000.html) in just over a week. Reuters says investors are worried about rising interest rates and the cost of building AI. A week earlier, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI, Anthropic's biggest rival, brings in [about $50 billion a year, not the $70 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/article/anthropic-q3-revenue-climbs-to-168-billion-report-221619202.html) investors had assumed.

Not everyone is worried. An early Anthropic investor told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/15/anthropic-investor-ipo-financials/) he still expects the IPO to price at around $1.8 trillion and climb from there. On the other side, independent research firm New Constructs thinks Anthropic is [worth just $150 billion](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/10/62239452/anthropic-ipo-could-be-2026s-most-ridiculous-offering-as-analyst-values-ai-firm-at-just-150-billion-unprecedented-test-of-investor-gullibility).

This matters beyond one company. OpenAI is still private, and investors there are reportedly paying $1.4 trillion. Soon, every private AI price like that will be compared with a public one. This week, that yardstick got shorter.

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Public investors dumped AI's landlords while private investors bid up its tenants

AI runs on chips in giant data centres. Some companies own those chips. Others rent them, or sit on top of the spending. This week, investors treated them very differently.

- **The landlord.** CoreWeave owns huge numbers of AI chips and rents them to AI companies. It bought those chips with borrowed money: about [$50 billion of debt](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/investing/2026/10/15/coreweave-shares-bonds-slide-further-as-ai-debt-fears-mount/), against roughly $13 billion in sales expected this year. This week, investors started to doubt it can pay that back. Its shares fell more than 15% in a single day, and its bonds began trading like those of a company in trouble. The same day, research firm Gartner warned that about four in five companies like CoreWeave could shut down by 2030.
- **A tenant.** [Modal](https://modal.com/) doesn't own chips. It rents computing power from more than 20 cloud providers and packages it so developers can run AI easily. It now brings in more than $1 billion a year. This week, it raised [$750 million at a $15.75 billion valuation](https://modal.com/blog/1-billion-annualized-run-rate-series-d). That's [more than three times](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/14/ai-cloud-startup-modal-raises-750m-at-15-75b-valuation/) what it was worth in May. Accel led the round, and Kleiner Perkins joined.
- **A toll booth.** Ramp gives companies corporate cards and software to track spending. It raised [$1.85 billion at a $60 billion valuation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/ramp-reaches-60b-valuation-as-it-pushes-deeper-into-ai/), up from $44 billion just four months ago. Investment firms Dragoneer, Thrive Capital and Founders Fund were among the leads. Ramp now sells a card built for AI agents, and early investor Keith Rabois told TechCrunch it will become the main way companies manage what they spend on AI.

All three depend on the same AI boom. But if demand cools, they get hit differently. The landlord still has to repay its lenders. The chips Modal uses belong to other companies. And Ramp sits on top of company spending, whoever the supplier is. **This week, the market paid for the meter, not the machine.**

CoreWeave's backers have a case. Evan Schlossman of Neostellar Capital, a listed fund that owns CoreWeave shares, told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/16/coreweave-investor-explains-ai-neocloud-debt/) that its customers are on <mark>"take-or-pay"</mark> contracts. That means they pay even if they don't use the capacity. CoreWeave says those contracts more than cover its debt. Still, PitchBook's John Atkins summed up the mood to [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/15/ai-coreweave-bonds-stocks): <mark>"people are taking a dim view of speculative borrowing that will need to be refinanced at punishingly high rates."</mark> For now, money is moving away from whoever carries the hardware debt.

</div>

### Siemens, AMD, Intel and Hitachi are writing the cheques for AI's physical bottlenecks

AI doesn't just need chips. It needs steady power, fast connections between chips and special materials. On the same Wednesday, three startups working on those problems raised money. Each round included a big industrial company.

- **Power.** AI chips can change how much electricity they draw in milliseconds, which strains a data centre's power system. Skeleton Technologies, from Estonia, makes energy-storage systems that smooth out those swings. Siemens, the German industrial group, led a round that values Skeleton at [more than $1 billion](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/skeleton-technologies-surpasses-1b-valuation-with-siemens-backing-for-us-expansion/).
- **Connections.** UK-based Salience Labs makes chips that use light instead of electricity to pass data between AI chips. Its [$125 million round](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/salience-labs-secures-125m-to-scale-photonic-chips-for-ai-data-centres/) was led by Bullhound Capital. Chipmakers AMD and Intel invested through their venture arms.
- **Materials.** Bengaluru's Scimplify makes high-purity specialty chemicals and materials, including ones used in chips and batteries. It does this through a network of more than 500 partner factories and brings in about $200 million a year. Hitachi Ventures led its [$25 million round](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/asia/hitachi-ventures-leads-25m-strategic-round-in-chemicals-platform-scimplify/), and Accel and 3one4 Capital invested again. One example shows its edge. A Japanese chip-materials company wanted a supplier outside China. Scimplify's sample passed its tests in two months, when the buyer had expected the process to take about a year.

Why would these giants invest? Each one is buying into a weak spot in its own business. Siemens, for example, sells electrical systems for data centres. Its executive Markus Grabmeier said, <mark>"Skeleton's portfolio perfectly complements Siemens' comprehensive data centre offering."</mark> Last edition, Samsung was doing the same with Indian chip startup Agrani Labs. A VC wants a return. A corporate also wants a place in the queue.

**VCs are betting on AI demand. Corporates are buying seats at its bottlenecks.** With chip stocks falling, corporate money is starting to look like the steadier kind for hardware startups. India has a real shot here. Hitachi Ventures' Radhika Ananth says much of India's factory capacity sits underused because it lacks the science and quality systems global buyers need. That's the gap Scimplify is filling.

## India, Inc.

### Zetwerk is listing below its last private price, and its VCs are selling more shares, not fewer
{: .take}

Zetwerk is a Bengaluru-based [contract manufacturer](https://yourstory.com/2021/07/zetwerk-contract-manufacturing-amrit-acharya), founded in 2018. Companies send it their designs, and Zetwerk gets the parts made through its network of factories, from pipes to aircraft engine parts. Its customers span oil and gas, renewable energy and electric vehicles. It's backed by VC firms including Lightspeed, Peak XV and Accel.

On 15 October, Zetwerk opened its ₹5,012 crore IPO. At the top of its price range, the IPO values the company at about ₹25,600 crore. In March, private investors valued it at ₹27,000 crore. So the IPO price is about 5% lower, [according to Mint](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:5d5270b1f7158:0-zetwerk-targets-rs-25-583-crore-valuation-in-rs-5-012-crore-ipo/). Mint notes the gap almost disappears if you leave out shares set aside for employees. Either way, there's no premium.

Even at that price, Zetwerk's investors want to sell more. Part of the IPO is an offer for sale, where existing shareholders sell their own shares. That part has grown [by about half](https://inc42.com/buzz/zetwerk-raises-%E2%82%B92251-cr-from-anchor-investors-ahead-of-ipo/) since the draft filing. Lightspeed, Peak XV, Accel and Kae Capital are among the sellers.

There are plenty of buyers, too. Before the IPO opened, big funds put in ₹2,251 crore. They include BlackRock, Norway's sovereign wealth fund and 22 Indian mutual fund companies. And the business is improving. Sales in the June quarter were 55% higher than a year earlier, and losses shrank.

Tonbo Imaging shows the same pattern. The Bengaluru company makes thermal cameras and weapon sights for armed forces. Its ₹852 crore IPO is entirely existing investors selling their shares. On day one, investors [asked for four times the shares on offer](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/tonbo-imaging-ipo-subscribed-4-times-on-day-1-led-by-strong-nii-demand-126101501609_1.html), even though Tonbo's revenue fell last year. Before the IPO, existing investors also sold ₹413 crore of shares at the top IPO price to Indian buyers, including 360 ONE's defence fund.

Compare that with private funding. Indian startups raised just [$113 million](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-oct-5-oct-10-12662210) from private investors in the week to 10 October. That's 72% less than the week before. And next week, [Jio Platforms](https://inc42.com/buzz/jio-ipo-to-open-for-subscription-on-october-21/), Reliance's telecom and technology arm, opens a [₹30,213 crore IPO](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/ipo-corner/story/jio-platforms-ipo-price-band-announced-at-rs-1065-1119-set-to-become-indias-biggest-public-issue-561665-2026-10-14). It's set to be India's largest ever. Right now, India's deepest pool of growth money isn't in VC funds. It's on the stock market, and much of it is Indian.

**A slightly lower price you can sell at beats a higher one you can't.**

### India's ₹1 lakh crore deeptech fund has picked its first 19 VCs, and it wants private money alongside every rupee
{: .take}

In November 2025, the government launched a ₹1 lakh crore fund to back research-heavy startups. It's called the Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) Fund. Last edition, chip startup Agrani Labs was applying for money from it. This week, the Economic Times reported who will hand most of that money out.

The fund picked its first [19 fund managers](https://www.inkl.com/news/blume-speciale-kalaari-chiratae-others-part-of-first-cohort-to-receive-rs-7-131-crore-rdi-fund) from more than 160 applicants. Together, they'll get ₹7,131 crore. Fourteen are VC funds, including Chiratae, Blume, Kalaari and Speciale Invest. Five are research organisations, including centres at IISc, IIT Madras and IIT Bombay.

There are two conditions. First, the managers can only back companies at technology readiness level 4 or above. That's a [1-to-9 scale](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/technology-readiness-level-trl) of how mature a technology is. Level 4 means its parts have been shown to work in a lab. Second, the managers must raise private money alongside the government's. The government's money also won't all arrive up front.

**The government isn't picking startups. It's picking the people who pick startups.** That's a sensible approach for a state that doesn't want to run a VC firm. And the matching rule means private investors have to say yes, too.

This is a start, not a flood. The first group gets about 7% of the full fund, and a second group is promised soon. There's a warning sign as well. The same ET report mentions an Indian Express investigation that raised conflict-of-interest questions about how another arm of the scheme picked projects. If the money moves on time and the managers find private partners, Indian deeptech gets patient money with a VC's discipline.
