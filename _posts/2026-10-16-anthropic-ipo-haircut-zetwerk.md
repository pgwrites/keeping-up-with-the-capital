Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about the investor day that took half a trillion dollars off the table.

Last week, I said the stock market was about to get a say in what frontier AI is worth. I didn't expect it to start before the IPO. This week, investors who have happily paid up for AI in private began asking tougher questions. Is growth slowing? Who's carrying the debt? ## The Big Sip

# Anthropic's sales grew 45% last quarter, but investors now want to value its IPO $500 billion lower
{: .take}

Moving fast and speeding up sound like the same thing. But they aren't, and this week, the difference cost [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) about $500 billion on paper.

Anthropic is preparing to go public. On Wednesday, it gave its biggest investors an update, [Reuters reported](https://wtvbam.com/2026/10/15/exclusive-investors-temper-anthropic-ipo-valuation-expectations-over-revenue-growth-slowdown-sources-say/). From July to September, it brought in $16.8 billion. That's 45% more than in the three months before. It also made a profit, by its own adjusted measure.

To put that in context, Anthropic made $4.6 billion in all of 2025. It now makes more than three times that in a single quarter.

So why the long faces? The trend. In the previous quarter, sales had more than doubled. This quarter, they grew 45%. That's still fast, but it's slower. According to Reuters' sources, some investors now want to value Anthropic at about $1.5 trillion in its IPO. Before this, they expected about $2 trillion. That's $500 billion less, before a single share has traded.

**Investors care less about how fast Anthropic is growing than about whether that growth is speeding up.**

Even $1.5 trillion is a steep price. It's about 22 times what Anthropic now earns in a year. And it's still well above the [$965 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/two-exit-doors-anthropic-2-051103937.html) investors valued it at in May.

The wider mood didn't help. US chip stocks lost [$1.64 trillion in value](https://www.aol.com/articles/us-chip-stocks-set-wipe-135038000.html) in just over a week. Reuters says investors are worried about rising interest rates and the cost of building AI. A week earlier, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI brings in [about $50 billion a year, not the $70 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/article/anthropic-q3-revenue-climbs-to-168-billion-report-221619202.html) investors had assumed.

Not everyone is worried. An early Anthropic investor told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/15/anthropic-investor-ipo-financials/) he still expects the IPO to price at around $1.8 trillion and climb from there. On the other side, independent research firm New Constructs thinks Anthropic is [worth just $150 billion](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/10/62239452/anthropic-ipo-could-be-2026s-most-ridiculous-offering-as-analyst-values-ai-firm-at-just-150-billion-unprecedented-test-of-investor-gullibility).

A lower price may even help. Michael Parekh, who ran Goldman Sachs' internet research in the 1990s, [wrote this week](https://michaelparekh.substack.com/p/show-and-tell-in-ai-anthropic-microsoft) that <mark>"an IPO priced to sell, that trades well afterwards, is often better than one priced at the peak."</mark>

This matters beyond one company. OpenAI is still private, and investors there are reportedly paying $1.4 trillion. Soon, every private AI price like that will be compared with a public one. This week, that yardstick got shorter.

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Investors punished CoreWeave for borrowing heavily to buy AI chips, while paying more for Modal and Ramp

This week, investors drew a sharp line between AI companies that carry heavy debt and those that don't.

- **CoreWeave borrowed heavily to buy AI chips.** [CoreWeave](https://www.coreweave.com) rents out computing power from AI chips it owns. It bought those chips with borrowed money: about [$50 billion of debt](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/investing/2026/10/15/coreweave-shares-bonds-slide-further-as-ai-debt-fears-mount/), against roughly $13 billion in sales expected this year. This week, investors started to doubt it can pay that back. Its shares fell more than 15% in a single day, and its bonds began trading like those of a company in trouble. Investor Chamath Palihapitiya [asked on X](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/equities/26/10/62345955/elon-musk-says-yikes-as-coreweave-bond-yield-hits-15-amid-chamath-palihapitiyas-private-credit-bubble-warning), <mark>"Is the AI buildout creating a private credit bubble?"</mark> Elon Musk replied with one word: <mark>"Yikes."</mark> The same day, Gartner warned that about four in five companies like CoreWeave could shut down by 2030.
- **Modal rents chips instead of owning them.** [Modal](https://modal.com/) rents computing power from more than 20 cloud providers and packages it so developers can run AI easily. It now brings in more than $1 billion a year. This week, it raised [$750 million at a $15.75 billion valuation](https://modal.com/blog/1-billion-annualized-run-rate-series-d). That's [more than three times](https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/14/ai-cloud-startup-modal-raises-750m-at-15-75b-valuation/) what it was worth in May. [Accel](https://www.accel.com) led the round, and [Kleiner Perkins](https://www.kleinerperkins.com) joined.
- **Ramp's software manages company spending, including on AI.** [Ramp](https://ramp.com) offers corporate charge cards, expense management and bill-payment software to companies. It raised [$1.85 billion at a $60 billion valuation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/ramp-reaches-60b-valuation-as-it-pushes-deeper-into-ai/), up from $44 billion just four months ago. [Dragoneer](https://www.dragoneer.com), [Thrive Capital](https://thrivecap.com) and [Founders Fund](https://foundersfund.com) were among the leads. Ramp now sells a card built for AI agents, and early investor Keith Rabois told TechCrunch it will become the main way companies manage what they spend on AI.

All three depend on the same AI boom. But if demand cools, they get hit differently. CoreWeave still has to repay its lenders. The chips Modal uses belong to other companies. And Ramp's business grows with company spending, whoever the supplier is. **This week, investors rewarded companies that make money from AI being used, and punished the one that borrowed to buy the hardware.**

CoreWeave's backers have a case. Evan Schlossman of [Neostellar Capital](https://neostellar.vc), a listed fund that owns CoreWeave shares, told [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/16/coreweave-investor-explains-ai-neocloud-debt/) that its customers are on <mark>"take-or-pay"</mark> contracts. That means they pay even if they don't use the capacity. CoreWeave says those contracts more than cover its debt. Still, PitchBook's John Atkins summed up the mood to [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/15/ai-coreweave-bonds-stocks): <mark>"people are taking a dim view of speculative borrowing that will need to be refinanced at punishingly high rates."</mark> For now, money is moving away from whoever carries the hardware debt.

### Siemens, AMD, Intel and Hitachi are investing in startups that fix AI data centres' power, networking and materials problems

On Wednesday, three startups working on the physical side of AI raised money. Each round included a big industrial company.

- **Power: Skeleton Technologies.** AI chips can change how much electricity they draw within milliseconds, which strains a data centre's power system. Estonia's [Skeleton Technologies](https://www.skeletontech.com) makes energy-storage systems that smooth out those swings. Siemens led a round that values it at [more than $1 billion](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/skeleton-technologies-surpasses-1b-valuation-with-siemens-backing-for-us-expansion/).
- **Networking: Salience Labs.** UK-based [Salience Labs](https://www.saliencelabs.ai) makes photonic chips, which use light instead of electricity to move data between AI chips across server racks. Its [$125 million round](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/salience-labs-secures-125m-to-scale-photonic-chips-for-ai-data-centres/) was led by [Bullhound Capital](https://bullhoundcapital.com). AMD and Intel invested through their venture arms.
- **Materials: Scimplify.** Bengaluru's [Scimplify](https://www.scimplify.com) makes high-purity specialty chemicals and materials, including ones used in chips and batteries. It does this through a network of more than 500 partner factories and brings in about $200 million a year. [Hitachi Ventures](https://www.hitachi-ventures.com) led its [$25 million round](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/asia/hitachi-ventures-leads-25m-strategic-round-in-chemicals-platform-scimplify/), and [Accel](https://www.accel.com) and [3one4 Capital](https://www.3one4capital.com) invested again. A Japanese chip-materials company wanted a supplier outside China. Scimplify's sample passed its tests in two months, when the buyer had expected the process to take about a year.

Each of these giants is backing a technology its own business needs. Siemens, for example, sells electrical systems for data centres. Its executive Markus Grabmeier [said](https://tech.eu/2026/10/14/skeleton-technologies-surpasses-1b-valuation-with-siemens-backing-for-us-expansion/), <mark>"Skeleton's portfolio perfectly complements Siemens' comprehensive data centre offering."</mark> Last edition, Samsung was doing the same with Indian chip startup [Agrani Labs](https://www.agranilabs.com/).

**VCs invest for financial returns. Corporates also invest to secure technology and suppliers their own businesses need.** With chip stocks falling, corporate money is starting to look like the steadier kind for hardware startups.

India could benefit. <mark>"Specialty chemicals demand has outgrown the models built to serve it,"</mark> Radhika Ananth, managing director of India at Hitachi Ventures, told [Global Corporate Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/asia/hitachi-ventures-leads-25m-strategic-round-in-chemicals-platform-scimplify/). She said much of India's factory capacity is underused and lacks the science and quality systems global buyers require. Scimplify is building a business to close that gap.

## India, Inc.

### Zetwerk's IPO values it below its last private round, yet its VC backers are selling more shares
{: .take}

[Zetwerk](https://www.zetwerk.com) is a Bengaluru-based [contract manufacturer](https://yourstory.com/2021/07/zetwerk-contract-manufacturing-amrit-acharya), founded in 2018. Companies send it their designs, and Zetwerk gets the parts made through its network of factories, from pipes to aircraft engine parts. Its customers span oil and gas, renewable energy and electric vehicles. It's backed by VC firms including [Lightspeed](https://lsvp.com), [Peak XV](https://www.peakxv.com) and [Accel](https://www.accel.com).

On 15 October, Zetwerk opened its ₹5,012 crore IPO. At the top of its price range, the IPO values the company at about ₹25,600 crore. In March, private investors valued it at ₹27,000 crore. So the IPO price is about 5% lower, [according to Mint](https://www.tradingview.com/news/moodys:5d5270b1f7158:0-zetwerk-targets-rs-25-583-crore-valuation-in-rs-5-012-crore-ipo/). Mint notes the gap almost disappears if you leave out shares set aside for employees. Either way, there's no premium.

Even at that price, Zetwerk's investors want to sell more. The number of shares existing investors are selling in the IPO has grown [by about half](https://inc42.com/buzz/zetwerk-raises-%E2%82%B92251-cr-from-anchor-investors-ahead-of-ipo/) since the draft filing. Lightspeed, Peak XV, Accel and [Kae Capital](https://www.kae-capital.com) are among the sellers.

Big funds are buying. Before the IPO opened, they put in ₹2,251 crore, including BlackRock, Norway's sovereign wealth fund and 22 Indian mutual fund companies. The wider market was cooler. On day one, investors bid for [less than a quarter of the shares on offer](https://inc42.com/buzz/zetwerk-ipo-subscribed-8-on-day-1-so-far/). That's despite an improving business: sales in the June quarter were 55% higher than a year earlier, and losses shrank.

[Tonbo Imaging](https://tonboimaging.com), a Bengaluru defence electronics maker, opened its IPO the same day and drew far more demand. Its ₹852 crore IPO is entirely existing investors selling their shares. On day one, investors [asked for four times the shares on offer](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/tonbo-imaging-ipo-subscribed-4-times-on-day-1-led-by-strong-nii-demand-126101501609_1.html), even though Tonbo's revenue fell last year. Before the IPO, existing investors also sold ₹413 crore of shares at the top IPO price to Indian buyers, including [360 ONE](https://www.360.one)'s defence fund.

Compare that with private funding. Indian startups raised just [$113 million](https://entrackr.com/report/weekly-funding-report-weekly-funding-report/funding-and-acquisitions-in-indian-startups-this-week-oct-5-oct-10-12662210) from private investors in the week to 10 October. That's 72% less than the week before. And next week, [Jio Platforms](https://inc42.com/buzz/jio-ipo-to-open-for-subscription-on-october-21/) opens a [₹30,213 crore IPO](https://www.businesstoday.in/markets/ipo-corner/story/jio-platforms-ipo-price-band-announced-at-rs-1065-1119-set-to-become-indias-biggest-public-issue-561665-2026-10-14). It's set to be India's largest ever. Right now, India's deepest pool of growth money isn't in VC funds. It's on the stock market, and much of it is Indian.

**A slightly lower price you can sell at beats a higher one you can't.**

### India's ₹1 lakh crore deeptech fund has picked its first 19 fund managers, and each must raise private money alongside it
{: .take}

In November 2025, the government launched a ₹1 lakh crore fund to back research-heavy startups. It's called the Research, Development and Innovation (RDI) Fund. Last edition, chip startup [Agrani Labs](https://www.agranilabs.com/) was applying for money from it. This week, the Economic Times reported who will hand most of that money out.

The fund picked its first [19 fund managers](https://www.inkl.com/news/blume-speciale-kalaari-chiratae-others-part-of-first-cohort-to-receive-rs-7-131-crore-rdi-fund) from more than 160 applicants. Together, they'll get ₹7,131 crore. Fourteen are VC funds, including [Chiratae](https://www.chiratae.com), [Blume](https://blume.vc), [Kalaari](https://kalaari.com) and [Speciale Invest](https://www.specialeinvest.com). Five are research organisations, including centres at IISc, IIT Madras and IIT Bombay.

There are two conditions. First, the managers can only back companies at technology readiness level 4 or above. That's a [1-to-9 scale](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/technology-readiness-level-trl) of how mature a technology is. Level 4 means its parts have been shown to work in a lab. Second, the managers must raise private money alongside the government's. The government's money also won't all arrive up front.

**The government isn't picking startups. It's picking the people who pick startups.** That's a sensible approach for a state that doesn't want to run a VC firm. And the matching rule means private investors have to say yes, too.

This is a start, not a flood. The first group gets about 7% of the full fund, and a second group is promised soon. There's a warning sign as well. The [same ET report](https://www.inkl.com/news/blume-speciale-kalaari-chiratae-others-part-of-first-cohort-to-receive-rs-7-131-crore-rdi-fund) mentions an Indian Express investigation that raised conflict-of-interest questions about how another arm of the scheme picked projects. If the money moves on time and the managers find private partners, Indian deeptech gets patient money with a VC's discipline.
