Hello there, stranger! 👀

Here for a sip?

Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about how Anthropic's first profit lasted exactly one quarter.

Last week, I asked what you read first when a company opens its books: the profit or the bills. This week, Anthropic answered for me. The bills won. But the reason surprised me: it had more customers than computing power. Elsewhere, the money got choosier. Sequoia shrank on purpose, OpenAI has raised less than half of what it asked for, and Razorpay is going public for less than it was worth in 2021. <mark>So when investors get pickier, who still gets paid?</mark>

Let's get to it.

## The Big Sip

# Anthropic's profit lasted one quarter, because demand outran the cheap computing power it had booked
{: .take}

Last week, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/)'s prospectus showed its first quarterly operating profit: $974 million, in the three months to June. On Monday, it [updated the filing](https://fortune.com/2026/11/02/anthropics-growth-slowed-and-costs-jumped-in-the-third-quarter-updated-ipo-filing-shows/) with its September quarter. The profit was gone. Anthropic made an operating loss of $567 million.

The reason is unusual. Anthropic didn't run short of customers. It ran short of computing power. It books most of its computing 12 to 18 months before it needs it, on multi-year contracts, [the filing says](https://ppc.land/anthropic-swings-to-567m-q3-operating-loss-on-5bn-of-short-term-compute/). When demand runs ahead of those contracts, it rents extra capacity at short notice. In the September quarter, it spent $5 billion renting that way, [Business Insider reported](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropics-amended-ipo-paperwork-reveals-153730799.html). That's almost nine times the size of the loss. And the filing says this computing "currently costs significantly more" than what it buys on long contracts.

<mark>"We have consistently operated in a compute-constrained environment where customer demand exceeds available compute,"</mark> the filing says, [as Forbes contributor Jon Markman quoted](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/11/03/anthropic-posts-a-567-million-operating-loss-from-buying-extra-compute/). Anthropic also expects its short-term buying to "continue to increase in the near term." Its fix is capacity of its own, leased and run for it, which it calls Anthropic Direct. As that comes online, it plans to swap the rented computing for cheaper long-term supply.

But supply isn't the whole story. Revenue grew 47% from the June quarter, to $17 billion. In the quarter before, it had grown 144%, [Fortune reported](https://fortune.com/2026/11/02/anthropics-growth-slowed-and-costs-jumped-in-the-third-quarter-updated-ipo-filing-shows/). Price cuts on new models and contract discounts also took about $600 million off revenue. <mark>"The operating line is where investors should focus,"</mark> Johan Shamshad wrote [on Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/analysis/anthropics-ipo-story-changed-with-the-amended-s1-200690787).

OpenAI had a harder week. It wanted $30 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation. It has about $11 billion committed and has cut its goal to $20 billion, [the Wall Street Journal reported](https://www.edwardconard.com/macro-roundup/openai-raises-less-than-half-of-30-billion-target-in-latest/). It now plans to raise $9 billion of that from family offices and wealthy individuals. Abu Dhabi's MGX is expected to be the biggest investor, and OpenAI would get an option to lease new computing capacity in the UAE, [per Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/guneyyildiz/2026/11/02/openai-cuts-round-to-20-billion-as-uae-investors-pair-capital-with-compute-capacity/).

Anthropic still hasn't set a price range. Bloomberg reported that its road show could start as early as the week of 9 November, [FinanceFeeds noted](https://financefeeds.com/anthropic-ipo-roadshow-looms-with-no-price-range/).

**Anthropic lost money because it had more customers than computers. OpenAI's problem is that it found fewer investors than it asked for.**

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Fal doubled its valuation in seven months because there isn't enough computing power to go round

Anthropic isn't the only company short of computing power. [Fal](https://fal.ai) runs AI models that make images, video and audio for other businesses, which reach them through a single connection in their own apps. Its customers include film studios, ad agencies and game makers.

This week, Fal raised $1.3 billion at a $16 billion valuation, led by [Bessemer Venture Partners](https://www.bvp.com), [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/03/ai-inference-startup-fal-doubles-its-valuation-to-16b-as-demand-surges). In April, it was worth $8 billion. Its yearly revenue passed $800 million earlier this year, per Bloomberg, so investors paid about 20 times revenue.

Demand explains the price. <mark>"We hit a point in summer where we were not able to serve our customers and had to rate-limit them,"</mark> co-founder and CEO Burkay Gur said, [per SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/03/generative-media-inference-startup-fal-raises-1-3b-at-a-16b-valuation/).

But the shortage cuts both ways. Companies like Fal also buy or lease the chips they run on. That's expensive, so gross margins across the sector remain thin, TechCrunch reported, citing people familiar with their finances.

India has a smaller version of the same story. [Last week](/keeping-up-with-the-capital/editions/2026-10-30-anthropic-s1-profit-compute-bills/), Bengaluru's [Simplismart](https://simplismart.ai), which helps companies run AI models faster and more cheaply, raised $14 million after its revenue run rate grew 14 times in six months.

**When computing power is scarce, investors pay most for whoever can get it to customers. But as long as it stays scarce, the companies renting out the chips keep the fattest margins.**

### The biggest VC firms are backing fewer companies: Sequoia shut London and its small-cheque programmes, and Disruptive raised $10 billion for about ten bets

On Wednesday, [Sequoia Capital](https://www.sequoiacap.com) posted a memo called "The Sequoia of Tomorrow". It has raised about $10 billion in new funds, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/sequoia-hits-rewind-undoing-20-years-of-expansion/). It also closed its London office and ended Arc, its accelerator for seed-stage founders. It ended Scouts, too, the programme that let founders and operators invest small sums on its behalf. Its early-stage and growth teams are now one team. <mark>"Our objective function is not scale, but quality,"</mark> the memo said.

Scouts had a famous record. Started around 2009, it got Sequoia into Uber and Stripe, [Dealroom noted](https://dealroom.co/news/170262-why-sequoia-ended-scouts-the-2009-programme-that-found-uber-and-stripe/). Dealroom also pointed out that in today's AI seed rounds, a $100,000 cheque buys far less access than it did then.

Indian readers have seen Sequoia pull back before. In 2023, its India and Southeast Asia arm split off and became [Peak XV Partners](/keeping-up-with-the-capital/memory/peak-xv-partners/), and its China arm became HongShan.

Disruptive is going the same way. Last week, the Dallas firm closed a $10 billion fund to put about $1 billion each into roughly ten late-stage companies in AI, defence and software, [The Next Web reported](https://thenextweb.com/news/disruptive-10bn-fund-ai-defence-startups), citing Bloomberg. Its earlier bets include Groq, ElevenLabs and Databricks. <mark>"We can't write small checks,"</mark> founder Alex Davis told Bloomberg.

**The biggest firms aren't raising less money. They're spreading it across fewer founders.**

## India, Inc.

### Razorpay is heading to the stock market at about a third below its 2021 valuation
{: .take}

[Razorpay](https://razorpay.com) processes online payments for businesses, and it moved its legal home back to India before listing. SEBI has approved its IPO, which could raise ₹5,000–6,000 crore, [Inc42 reported](https://inc42.com/features/mdr-and-ai-inside-the-two-acronyms-that-could-shape-razorpays-%e2%82%b96000-cr-ipo/). Nearly half of that is expected to go to existing shareholders selling shares. Razorpay is reportedly targeting a valuation of about $5 billion. In December 2021, private investors valued it at $7.5 billion.

The business is still growing fast. Revenue rose about 65% in FY25, to ₹3,783 crore. But it slipped into a loss that year, mainly because of ₹1,209 crore in employee stock costs and one-time charges. The move back to India also came with a reported tax bill of about ₹1,245 crore.

There's a possible boost ahead. A 0.4% fee on large UPI payments is due after Diwali, and about half of Razorpay's payments run through UPI. Analysts estimate the fee could add ₹192–461 crore a year to its revenue, Inc42 reported. But how much Razorpay keeps depends on revenue-sharing deals with banks that aren't settled yet. PhonePe has revived its own IPO plans on the back of the same fee, and is reportedly targeting a valuation of about $10 billion.

[Last week](/keeping-up-with-the-capital/editions/2026-10-30-anthropic-s1-profit-compute-bills/), a Baillie Gifford fund's books valued VerSe, the parent of Dailyhunt, about 90% below its 2022 price. VerSe pushed back and said other investors still value it at its old price. **VerSe is defending its 2022 price in private. Razorpay is accepting a lower one in public.**

### NBCUniversal co-led an $80 million round in EloElo, an Indian microdrama app that makes each show for less than $15,000
{: .take}

Microdramas are short dramas made to be watched on a phone, episode by episode. EloElo, a Bengaluru company started in 2020 by former Flipkart executive Saurabh Pandey, runs a microdrama app called Story TV. It has more than 5,000 shows in six languages, and users spend 95 minutes a day on it, the company says.

This week, EloElo raised $80 million, co-led by NBCUniversal and Hornbill Capital, [Business Standard reported](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/eloelo-series-c-funding-hornbill-nbcuniversal-story-tv-microdrama-126110400565_1.html). The round reportedly values it at about $500 million.

The cost of making each show explains the interest. EloElo now makes 250 new dramas a month, and the average one costs well under $15,000. AI handles 70–75% of the work, and some shows are made entirely with AI. <mark>"Story TV shows us what India wants to watch, and Dolphin AI gives us the economics to make it,"</mark> Pandey said. Dolphin AI is the company's own AI production suite.

The audience is paying, too. Redseer estimates that 40 million Indians paid for a microdrama at least once in FY26. Story TV competes with [Kuku](https://kukufm.com)'s Kuku TV and JioHotstar's Tadka. EloElo wants to take the format abroad, aiming at diaspora and local-language audiences. NBCUniversal says it will help, including in the US.

**NBCUniversal isn't paying for one hit show. It's paying for a way to make 250 shows a month.**
