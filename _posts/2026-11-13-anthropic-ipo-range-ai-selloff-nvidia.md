Hello there, stranger! 👀

Here for a sip?

Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about how Anthropic asked for less, and got mobbed anyway.

This was a strange week to sell AI to the stock market. AI stocks had just had their worst day in over a year. Then, on Thursday, Nvidia's founder resigned. And yet Anthropic's IPO drew about six times the orders it needed, and a British chip startup that hasn't shipped a chip yet was valued at $7 billion. <mark>So is the money running away from AI, or just getting fussier about the price?</mark>

Let's get to it.

## The Big Sip

# Anthropic set its IPO price below the hype in the worst week for AI stocks, and drew about six times the orders it needed
{: .take}

A few months ago, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/) was reportedly aiming for a valuation of about $2 trillion. Then AI stocks crashed. On 5 November, after the Democrats' big wins in the US midterm elections, the Nasdaq fell 5.5%. It was its worst day since April 2025, [Reuters reported](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/international/4002398-investors-wipe-25-trillion-off-nasdaq-as-blue-tsunami-prompts-ai-fears). Investors feared the new Congress would slow down data centre building. <mark>"The market's moving from the promise of AI to the price of AI,"</mark> said Ted Parkhill, founder and CEO of Incline Investment Management.

That's the market Anthropic walked into. On Monday night, it sent bankers a price range of $70 to $80 a share. Then it emailed again to say the figures were "inaccurate", [Reuters reported](https://investinglive.com/stocks/anthropic-retracts-ipo-pricing-details-sent-to-bankers-says-figures-were-inaccurate/). Some big investors wanted a valuation below the $1.5 trillion that had been expected, per the Wall Street Journal.

On Tuesday, Anthropic settled on $70 to $78. At the top of that range, it would be worth $1.4 trillion and raise up to $64 billion on Nasdaq, under the ticker ANT, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-ipo-price-range-of-70-to-78-per-share-to-reach-an-implied-valuation-of-1-404-trillion/). That's about 45% more than the $965 billion it was valued at privately in May. But it's about 30% below the $2 trillion talk.

The lower price worked. By Thursday, orders were roughly six times the shares on offer, [Bloomberg reported](https://www.siliconrepublic.com/business/bloomberg-anthropic-ipo-six-times-oversubscribed-ai). The bankers now expect to price above the range, according to the Financial Times, [per Seoul Economic Daily](https://en.sedaily.com/world/2026/11/12/anthropic-ipo-demand-swamps-order-book-may-top-spacex-record). Trading could start on 19 November.

Some of the biggest buyers are Anthropic's own partners. Seven companies, led by Google and Nvidia with about $10 billion each, will buy $26 billion of shares at the IPO price, [per TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-ipo-price-range-of-70-to-78-per-share-to-reach-an-implied-valuation-of-1-404-trillion/). With the public offering, that's up to [$90.4 billion](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/anthropic-ipo-reportedly-oversubscribed-company-143329410.html). SpaceX's record IPO in June raised [$85.7 billion](https://en.sedaily.com/world/2026/11/12/anthropic-ipo-demand-swamps-order-book-may-top-spacex-record).

Not everyone is reassured. <mark>"An underperforming Anthropic IPO has to be a very prominent player in what will be a pretty massive correction,"</mark> Jack Selby, a managing partner at Peter Thiel's family office, told Bloomberg TV, [as ZeroHedge reported](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/anthropic-bankers-push-higher-ipo-valuation-thiel-capitals-jack-selby-warns-weak-debut). Big order books have misled before: Klarna and StubHub were heavily oversubscribed, and both still traded below their offer prices.

OpenAI also took a lower price this week. It closed about $11 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, below the $1.4 trillion it first sought, [TechBriefly reported](https://techbriefly.com/2026/11/09/openai-raises-11-billion-at-125-trillion-valuation/).

**The sell-off didn't kill the demand for AI's biggest names. It only lowered the price.**

## Connecting the Dots

<div class="pattern" markdown="1">

### Nvidia lost its founder in the same week investors valued a rival chip startup at $7 billion before it had shipped a single chip

On Thursday, Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO, [effective immediately](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-CEO-Transition/). He co-founded the company in 1993. His exit followed reports of two extramarital affairs, one with a woman accused of spying for China, [Inc42 reported](https://inc42.com/buzz/jensen-huang-resigns-as-ceo-of-chipmaker-nvidia/). Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim CEO. Nvidia's shares were down about 4% on Thursday, [per Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/colette-kress-named-nvidia-interim-ceo-jensen-huangs-abrupt-resignation-raises-investor-concerns). Michael Landsberg, chief investment officer at Landsberg Bennett Private Wealth Management, called the resignation <mark>"the worst-case scenario during this entire saga."</mark>

Why does one CEO matter so much? Because Nvidia isn't only a chip seller. It's in Anthropic's IPO with about $10 billion. It has offered credit support to the companies that rent out its chips. The Economist has called it the "central bank of AI", [Inc42 noted](https://inc42.com/?p=579810).

The day before, a London startup called [Fractile](https://fractile.ai) raised $720 million at a $7 billion valuation, [The Next Web reported](https://thenextweb.com/news/fractile-7bn-valuation-720m-ai-inference-chips). Fractile, founded in 2022 by Oxford robotics researcher Walter Goodwin, designs chips for running AI models once they're trained. Its bet is that the bottleneck is memory, not raw computing power, so it stacks memory and logic on top of each other. In May, it was worth about $1 billion. Its first chip isn't due until 2027. [Thrive Capital](https://thrivecap.com), [Founders Fund](https://foundersfund.com), [Lightspeed](https://lsvp.com) and Redpoint led the round.

The customer explains much of the price. Anthropic has reportedly placed an initial order worth about $250 million, Bloomberg reported, [per Business Model Analyst](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/fractile-7-billion-valuation-anthropic-order/). Thrive's Philip Clark said the market for running AI models is <mark>"at least as large as the market Nvidia is addressing today."</mark> And Anthropic's own $26 billion share sale includes Nvidia's rivals AMD and Broadcom, alongside Nvidia.

**The more the AI economy depends on one company, the more investors will pay for a second option.**

### British state funds co-led two of the week's biggest startup rounds, in drones and AI chips

[UFORCE](https://www.uforce.com) was founded in Ukraine and is now based in London. It builds uncrewed boats, aircraft and ground vehicles, plus the software that controls them. On Thursday, it raised $700 million at a $6 billion valuation, [the company announced](https://www.uforce.com/news/uforce-raises-700m-at-6-billion-valuation-from-leading-tech-and-sovereign-wealth-funds-to-scale-combat-proven-defence-systems-for-ukraine-and-allied-nations). In March, it was valued at just over $1 billion.

Look at who led. Valor Equity Partners co-led with the UK's National Wealth Fund, a state-owned investor. The fund put in $150 million, its largest defence investment, Bloomberg reported, [per Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/uforce-raises-700m-at-6b-valuation-as-uk-wealth-fund-makes-its-biggest-defence-deal/). The US-Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund also joined. It called this its largest investment in emerging technology so far. The UK fund says UFORCE's plans will bring manufacturing and engineering jobs to Britain.

Fractile's round had state money in it, too. Britain's Sovereign AI fund and the British Business Bank both invested, [Reuters reported](https://sg.news.yahoo.com/uk-backed-ai-chip-startup-145142457.html).

India has a smaller version of the same mix. Pune's Greenjoules turns farm waste into renewable fuels. This week, it raised a ₹150 crore Series B, co-led by two Amazon funds, and a separate ₹100 crore loan from the Technology Development Board, a government body, [Entrepreneur India reported](https://india.entrepreneur.com/business-news/greenjoules-raises-inr-150-cr-series-b-secures-inr-100-cr-tdb-loan). The money goes into a plant meant to produce 200,000 litres of biocrude a day.

**Governments used to set the rules for startups and stand aside. In defence and AI, they're now writing cheques next to the VCs.**

## India, Inc.

### India's listed AI cloud companies have lost more than a third of their value since their highs, pulled down by a sell-off that started in the US
{: .take}

Neoclouds are companies that buy AI chips and rent out the computing power. In the US, CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN have been falling since September, as investors worry about AI spending and debt. The selling has now reached India's listed versions, [Inc42 reported](https://inc42.com/?p=579810).

The numbers are sharp. E2E Networks closed at about ₹491 on 9 November, down 36% from its October high. ESDS fell about 38% from its September high. That's even though E2E's revenue jumped more than five times in the September quarter, from a year earlier.

The difference is the customer base. US neoclouds have big tech companies signed up as anchor customers. Indian ones mostly don't. Yotta gets 75–80% of its business from international customers, and is targeting a $1.5 billion IPO, partly to repay debt. Government demand is less steady than it looks: the IndiaAI Mission used less than half of its ₹10,000 crore budget for FY26, per Neysa's blog. And the biggest anchor deal coming is foreign. CoreWeave is set to be the only tenant of a planned 240 MW AdaniConneX campus.

The money is getting stricter. Lenders and investors now favour companies with confirmed customers and large upfront payments, Inc42 reported. ESDS, for one, commits to spending only after a customer has paid at least 35% of a project's cost. <mark>"I would separate technology dependence from financial dependence,"</mark> said its founder, Piyush Somani.

**The US sell-off didn't change what India's neoclouds sell. It changed what lenders want to see before paying for the chips.**

### Two of India's 2021 favourites closed their chapters this week: Zupee quietly wound down, and Unacademy's founder stepped aside
{: .take}

Zupee made mobile versions of Ludo, Snakes and Ladders and Carrom that people played for money. It raised more than $120 million from investors including [Z47](https://www.z47.com) and WestCap. In August 2025, Parliament [banned real-money games](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/21/as-india-bans-real-money-games-dream-sports-mpl-start-pulling-the-plug), and Zupee stopped its paid games straight away.

In October, Zupee said it was leaving gaming to focus on AI. But the announcement came with its third and final round of layoffs, [Inc42 reported](https://inc42.com/buzz/why-zupees-ai-pivot-was-a-smokescreen-for-its-shutdown/). Headcount had peaked at close to 500. Sources told Inc42 that only three or four people now remain with the founders, and that the AI pivot covered a wind-down. Inc42 hasn't verified that, and Zupee and its investors didn't respond. How much cash is left is unknown.

Unacademy's ending is gentler. In September, upGrad bought it in an all-stock deal that valued it at just over $200 million, about 94% below its 2021 peak of $3.4 billion. On Wednesday, co-founder Gaurav Munjal moved from CEO to executive chairman, and Akhilendra Kumar took over as CEO, [Business Today reported](https://www.businesstoday.in/latest/corporate/story/gaurav-munjal-steps-down-as-unacademy-ceo-akhilendra-kumar-takes-charge-566923-2026-11-11). Munjal said he'll help with upGrad's planned IPO.

**Unacademy's investors swapped a markdown for upGrad shares. Zupee's lost their market to a law, and are still waiting to hear what's left.**
