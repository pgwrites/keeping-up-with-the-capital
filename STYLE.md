# Keeping Up with the Capital: editorial rules

Every edition follows these rules. Read this file in full before researching or writing. If anything here conflicts with an instruction in a specific request from PG (the owner), the request wins for that edition only.

## 1. What this publication is

- A weekly digest about venture capital and startups, global in scope, with India as the lens.
- The question every edition answers: **what is the money telling us this week?** Not "what happened in VC", but what the deals, valuations, exits and people moves signal.
- Reader: someone who recently got interested in VC and Indian startups. They know how venture works (rounds, valuations, dilution) and everyday startup terms (ARR, burn, Series A). They don't yet know most companies, funds or investors by name. They can follow one number at a time, but not several at once, and need to be told what a number means: is it big, small, good or bad? They respond best to plain words, short direct sentences and a warm, engaging tone.
- Published every Friday at 6 PM IST. Each edition covers Saturday to Friday of that week.

## 2. Structure (in this order)

1. **Greeting** (fixed opening, no heading):
   - "Hello there, stranger! 👀"
   - "Here for a sip?"
   - "Your favourite tea from Sand Hill Road to Koramangala is poured, and this week, everyone's whispering about…" (finish the line for the week)
   - Then one short paragraph in the first person that frames the week as a question worth caring about, ending with "Let's get to it."
2. **The Big Sip**: the one story or tension that defines the week. The headline is the page's only `#` (H1) heading. About 300–400 words.
3. **Connecting the Dots**: two or three patterns built from several stories. This is the signature section. Investor theses, exits and people moves live here when they support a pattern. If a pattern doesn't make a reader think "huh", don't run it.
4. **India, Inc.**: two or three insight-led stories. Lead with the insight, use market data as supporting context. Interesting IPOs are written up in prose (what happened, the numbers, why it matters), never as a table.
5. **The Week in Numbers**: three or four one-line benchmarks (see section 12).
6. **The Deal Sheet**: deals not already covered elsewhere in the edition.
7. **Watchlist**: three or four open questions for the coming weeks. Questions, not predictions.
8. **Rabbit Holes**: one featured read plus two to four bulleted links.
9. **Sources**: the collapsible "+ Sources" list of every link used.

Skip any section that has nothing good that week. Never pad.

The sign-off ("That's all for the week. See you at 6 PM IST, next Friday.") and the footer come from the layout. Don't write them in the edition file.

## 3. Voice

Write like a curious, well-read insider talking to a smart friend: warm, first-person and openly enthusiastic about how the money works. The reader should feel a person is walking them through the week, not a report summarising it. Human first, but every claim still sourced and every number checked.

- **Write in the first person and own the opinions,** but state them directly. Opinions follow the evidence and stay directional (see section 4).
- **No announcement lines.** Don't announce a point before making it ("Here's how I think about it", "Here's the part I find fascinating", "I'd go a step further", "Here's the kicker", "Now compare…"). Just make the point.
- **One idea per sentence.** Lead with the point, then add the number that proves it. Break long, winding sentences into short ones that follow each other smoothly and tell the story in order.
- **Fewer numbers, explained.** At most two numbers in a sentence and three or four in a paragraph. Keep only the numbers the story needs, round them ("three times", not "3.39x"), and say what each one means. Before a data-heavy passage, ask: what is the one story these numbers tell? Write that sentence first.
- **Give context only for early-stage companies.** For a seed to Series B startup, say in one plain sentence what it does. Assume the reader already knows later-stage and well-known companies, funds and institutions (OpenAI, Anthropic, Siemens, Reliance, Jio, SEBI, CoreWeave, Kalshi and the like); explaining them reads as padding. Exception: when a story is built around one company the reader may not know, give two or three sentences of background before the news. When a company's description is needed, make it comprehensive and specific, e.g. "Ramp offers corporate charge cards, expense management and bill-payment software to companies", not "Ramp gives companies cards and software". Use descriptors for funds only when verified.
- **Explain only highly technical concepts** (e.g. "technology readiness level", "photonic chips", "take-or-pay contracts"). Never explain basics the reader already knows, such as what AI needs to run, what a family office or offer for sale is, or what a global capability centre does.
- **Set up, then pay off.** A one-line setup is welcome only when it carries an idea of its own: "A price and a bid are different things." "If one set of deals explains software in 2026, it's this one." Don't stack setups; one per story at most.
- **Use analogies sparingly.** Only when a plain explanation can't do the job, and never stretch one across a story or section (no "landlords and tenants", "climbers on a mountain"). A direct statement is almost always better.
- **Land each story on a memorable line** the reader could repeat at dinner, written in plain, literal words: "Salesforce paid for future growth. Bending Spoons paid for today's cash flow." Bold it only when it is the single takeaway of the story, and at most once per story.
- **Talk to the reader.** Questions to the reader ("Why would family money step in where VCs stepped back?") and direct asides ("If you read one thing this weekend, make it this.") are encouraged, in moderation.
- **Report from sources, not access.** Every edition is built from public, secondary sources. First-person opinions and analysis are welcome, but never imply conversations, interviews or access that didn't happen ("a question I love asking founders", "LPs tell me", "we learned"). Credit the outlet instead ("the Economic Times reported").
- **Punctuate for the ear.** Put a comma after introductory words and phrases ("This week, …", "On Wednesday, …", "Last week, …"). Signal a contrast with a connector such as "But" instead of leaving the reader to infer it. Split any sentence that strings together three or more clauses. Not "Being fast and speeding up sound like the same thing. They aren't, and this week the difference cost Anthropic about $500 billion on paper." But "Moving fast and speeding up sound like the same thing. But they aren't, and this week, the difference cost Anthropic about $500 billion on paper."
- **Curious and optimistic, not cynical.** Find what is interesting in the week, including the bad news. No sneering at companies or people.
- **Headings must explain themselves.** Titles, subtitles, bullet labels and bold takeaways must be clear, direct and descriptive, so a reader who only skims them still gets what is being said. Make them arguments, not labels ("A $30 million-revenue AI startup sold for more than Airtable", not "Software valuations"), and keep jargon and metaphors out of them (not "Corporates are buying seats at its bottlenecks").
- **No italics.** Never italicise. To emphasise a phrase, highlight it with `<mark>`.
- Observation, then explanation, then implication. Show why a conclusion follows, and end each story on the "so what".
- Specific numbers instead of adjectives, within the limits above. Do the arithmetic for the reader only when the result is the point, and say it in words ("more than three times what it made in all of last year"). Compute it; never estimate in your head.
- Explain the counterintuitive part; that's usually the insight.
- Simple words. Mix short punchy sentences with longer explanatory ones so it reads like speech.
- Gossip is seasoning. The greeting carries most of it; the body can have a light touch.
- No corporate language (unlock, leverage, robust, seamless, transformative), no hype words and no filler. Warmth comes from the person behind the sentences, not from padding: if a sentence adds no idea, no setup and no feeling, cut it.
- Don't write "Translation:", "In other words" or "Quick primer". Just write the explanation as the next sentence.
- Don't explain basic VC or business terms (round, valuation, Series A, ARR, IPO, offer for sale, family office).

## 4. Opinions: directional calls yes, forecasts no

- Allowed: directional calls about where things are moving, placed after the evidence and woven into the narrative ("Family money is moving from the back of the cap table to the front").
- Not allowed: forecasts of outcomes, sizes or dates ("Anthropic will list above $2 trillion"), claims about what "everyone" thinks, mocking named people.
- Conditionals are fine when grounded ("If Agrani's round closes with the government money attached, …").
- Separate what is known from what is inferred. Attribute claims to the person or outlet that made them.
- Never present a call in a separate box or label. Blend it into the paragraph.

## 5. Sources, voices and fact-checking

- Every fact links to its source. Open and read the source before using it. Never state a number you haven't seen in a source or computed from sourced numbers.
- Prefer primary sources (company announcements, filings, data providers) and reputable reporting (Reuters, Bloomberg, TechCrunch, Inc42, Entrackr, Economic Times, Moneycontrol, Crunchbase News, PitchBook, Dealroom, Newcomer, Fortune).
- Aggregate many voices: investors, technologists, operators and VC enthusiasts. Investor newsletters, fund memos, podcasts with transcripts and blogs (e.g. Tomasz Tunguz, Not Boring, Stratechery) all count. X and LinkedIn posts only when quoted in an article or pasted in by PG.
- Include a well-known investor's or builder's opinion only when it adds to the story: it explains a number, challenges the main call, or raises something the facts alone don't. Never add a quote just to show a famous name reacted. One or two strong voices per story beat a roundup.
- When a call is contested, include the strongest sourced counter-voice.
- When quoting a person, give one or two lines of credentials, blended into the sentence.
- When reporting what a person said or thinks, quote them directly where possible and link the source at that point. Never paraphrase someone's view without a link.
- Unconfirmed figures: say "reportedly" in the sentence. No asterisks or footnotes.
- Use descriptors for firms and people only when verified.
- Verify dates and sequences ("four days later", "eight months on") against the sources.
- Proofread for grammar and punctuation (see section 3) before publishing.

## 6. The narrative test

Every detail must serve its story's headline. If a fact is true, verified and interesting but doesn't support the point, cut it. Each story appears once per edition; other sections may refer to it but never repeat it.

## 7. India rules

- An Indian startup is one headquartered in India or built out of India. Mark flipped companies. SiMa.ai does not count.
- Any story led by an Indian company, Indian founder or India-focused fund goes in India, Inc. Cross-market patterns stay in Connecting the Dots and may mention the Indian angle.
- Add an India angle to a global story only when there is a concrete link: an Indian investor, competitor, customer, policy or comparable deal. Never force it.

## 8. The Deal Sheet format

```html
<li><span class="dco"><a href="COMPANY_SITE">Company</a></span><span class="dmeta">Round · $Amount at $Valuation · Lead investors</span><span class="dsig">What the product does, in plain words. Then the founder, traction or signal that makes the deal worth noticing, with a source link.</span></li>
```

- Two or three lines per deal. Product first, then the interesting signal.
- Always link every startup and fund to its official website on first mention, once the URL is verified (in the body and in the Deal Sheet, including investors in the meta line).
- Leave out any part of the meta line that isn't disclosed rather than writing "Not disclosed".

## 9. Rabbit Holes

- Published within the last seven days. Cherry-pick; skip the section if nothing is good.
- One featured read with a one- or two-line note on why it's worth the time.
- Bullets: descriptive link text only. Don't name the publication or the date.
- At least half the picks should be global, not India-only.

## 10. The memory rule

- `_data/memory.yml` records every investor, company and theme in each edition, with the edition numbers it appeared in.
- The count is the number of **editions**, never the number of mentions.
- An entity appears in the sidebar and gets a page in `_memory/` only once it has appeared in **two or more editions**.
- When an entity reaches two editions, create `_memory/<slug>.md` (template below) and add `page: <slug>` to its entry. Update existing memory pages with each new appearance.
- The sidebar hides the Memory section entirely until at least one entity qualifies. Don't add any reader-facing note explaining the memory rule; it lives here and on the methodology page only.

Memory page template:

```markdown
---
title: "Agrani Labs"
description: "Every mention of Agrani Labs in Keeping Up with the Capital."
---

One or two plain sentences on who they are.

- **Edition 001 (9 October 2026):** what happened, with a source link. [Read the edition](/keeping-up-with-the-capital/editions/2026-10-09-anthropic-ipo-family-offices/)
- **Edition 002 (…):** …
```

## 11. Weekly procedure

1. Work out the coverage window: the Saturday to Friday ending on publication day (IST).
2. Read `SOURCES.md` and follow it in order: the forwards inbox, the core feed sweep, the numbers, then search to fill gaps. Research across global and Indian sources, plus investor and operator commentary. Keep notes of every source URL you open, and of any feed that failed to load.
3. Pick the Big Sip and the patterns first, then the India stories, then the Deal Sheet. Apply the narrative test.
4. Write the edition to `_posts/YYYY-MM-DD-short-keyword-slug.md` with this front matter:

```yaml
---
title: "The Big Sip headline, as an argument"
description: "One or two sentences summarising the edition's main stories, for search results."
edition_no: "002"
date_label: "16th October 2026"
card_title: "Short sidebar title, under 40 characters"
covers: "10 October to 16 October 2026"
---
```

   Mirror `_posts/2026-10-09-anthropic-ipo-family-offices.md` for the body markup (intro div, `{: .take}` headings, `pattern` divs, Deal Sheet list, Watchlist, Rabbit Holes, Sources).
5. Fact-check every number and date against its source, then proofread.
6. Update `_data/memory.yml` and any memory pages, following the memory rule.
7. Move the processed items in `_inbox/forwards.md` from "New" to "Done", as `SOURCES.md` describes.
8. Commit to `main` with the message "Edition NNN: <headline>" and push. GitHub Pages publishes within a few minutes.
9. In the run's summary, list any core feed that failed to load.

## 12. The Week in Numbers

A small block of benchmarks, so each edition leaves a record of the market week to week. It goes between India, Inc. and The Deal Sheet.

```markdown
## The Week in Numbers

- **$236 million** raised by 25 Indian startups (12–16 October), almost double the week before, [per Inc42](LINK).
- …
```

- Three or four bullets, one line each. Lead with the bold number, then say in plain words what it means.
- Always include: the Indian weekly total and deal count, with the change from the week before, from Inc42 (see `SOURCES.md` for timing and for why Inc42 wins over Entrackr). Give the dates the figure covers.
- Then pick one or two from: the week's biggest global round (Crunchbase News's weekly list), the biggest Indian round, the leading sector by money raised, or a new quarterly figure from a data report (Crunchbase, PitchBook, Carta, Tracxn, Blume, Bain) published that week.
- Every number links to its source and is copied from it, not estimated. Round as section 3 says.
- No commentary beyond the one line. If a number belongs in a story, use it there and pick another here; nothing appears twice (section 6).
- Skip the block if the Inc42 figure can't be found.
